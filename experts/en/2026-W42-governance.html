Expert view · Governance · Week 2026-W42 · 12 October 2026 – 18 October 2026

Legitimacy Under Strain: Elections, Exclusion and the Limits of Goodwill

Based on 31 reports and articles from 24 organisations.

Share:XFacebookTelegramWhatsApp

In short: UN briefers and think tanks described a transition advancing on paper toward elections and institution-building while economic strain, exclusionary policies toward women and Kurds, and unresolved Sweida tensions undercut public trust. Several sources warned that cooperation with the new authorities does not equate to confidence in state institutions, even as Damascus courts regional partners from Baghdad to Sanaa.

Findings

At the Security Council on 12 October, Deputy Special Envoy Claudio Cordone briefed on the political situation, while UN News summarized the session as covering economic strain, Israeli incursions, Sweida tensions and progress toward elections. A separate UN briefing noted a deputy envoy's call, reported by UN News in Arabic, for the international community to turn goodwill into tangible benefits rather than rhetoric. On the humanitarian track, Mohamed Yahya briefed on behalf of the Emergency Relief Coordinator on a deteriorating humanitarian situation, underscoring that political progress has not translated into improved conditions on the ground.

A ReliefWeb multi-site study on governance and civic participation examined local governance and peacebuilding dynamics across Syria during the transition, implicitly measuring how far decentralised structures are functioning. Etana think tank argued that institutional legitimacy in post-Assad Syria is complex, stressing that cooperation with authorities does not equal trust in state institutions, a distinction it says observers often conflate. Together these sources suggest the transitional government may be achieving procedural compliance without winning the deeper confidence of communities.

On the constitutional and electoral track, UNESCWA presented two studies, one on constitutional alternatives for Syria and another on the foundations of future elections, signalling technical groundwork is underway even as political consensus remains elusive. The Atlantic Council think tank argued Syria should avoid ethno-sectarian power-sharing arrangements, warning that such models produced deadlock and violence in Iraq and Lebanon and should not be replicated. This places the Atlantic Council in some tension with Kurdish and minority voices pressing for guaranteed representation rather than a purely civic model.

On gender exclusion, Human Rights Watch reported that Syrian authorities are barring women from defense and security colleges, framing this as a rollback of rights under the new government. The Syria Report think tank similarly noted growing criticism of restrictions on women's admission to some new universities and institutes, while Al-Jumhuriya outlet documented both old and new discriminatory policies in higher education, suggesting continuity as much as change in exclusionary practice. These sources converge on the view that gatekeeping in education and security institutions is emerging as an early marker of the transition's character.

On the Kurdish file, Rudaw thinktank reported Kurdish leaders discussing Kurdish rights with SDF commander Mazloum Abdi, while ENKS insisted that any meetings concerning Kurds must be inclusive and balanced, implicitly criticising exclusionary formats. Kurdpress covered disputes over northeastern Syria's parliamentary elections and Kurdish rights, and a separate Kurdpress item cited an official claiming Syrian Kurds prefer Bashar al-Assad to Erdogan, a provocative framing reflecting Kurdish-Turkish friction rather than a verified consensus view. A decision pending from the Ministry of Higher Education, per Aks al-Sair, on the status of northern Syria's universities and degrees adds another dimension to the integration question.

On the Autonomous Administration's institutional standing, The Syrian Observer examined renewed calls for dialogue in Suweida and asked whether a political settlement is within reach, echoing the Security Council's own concern about Sweida tensions. Regional diplomacy continued in parallel: Ard Ashur reported Iraq's president received an invitation from Ahmed al-Sharaa to visit Syria, and Iraqi News Agency reported discussions on strengthening Syrian-Lebanese-Iraqi coordination. JNS.org, a pro-Israel outlet, argued al-Sharaa's engagement with Yemen shows Syria seeking to sell security cooperation rather than simply receive aid, a framing that casts Damascus as an emerging regional security actor.

On accountability and transitional justice, the BBC reported that SOS Children's Villages acknowledged over 140 children in Syria were taken to its facilities by security services, amid a probe into disappearances, a finding with direct bearing on institutional trust and past-regime complicity. Plan International addressed the Security Council calling for urgent access to education for children, linking humanitarian and governance concerns. Separately, Al Akhbar reported a committee tracking Lebanese displaced from Syria criticising state neglect of their case, pointing to gaps in cross-border accountability mechanisms.

Social and cultural signals of political realignment also featured: France 24 reported concerts by singers accused of supporting the former Assad regime were cancelled after public backlash, illustrating informal social enforcement of transitional norms. Al-Jumhuriya examined inheritance disputes between Syrians who stayed and those who emigrated, a governance-adjacent property issue. Syria Direct quoted journalist Anand Gopal arguing Syria's unfinished revolution and democratic experiments hold broader lessons despite the challenges facing new leadership.

Where they agree

UN briefers, Etana and the ReliefWeb governance study broadly agree that procedural progress, on elections, constitutional studies and institution-building, has not yet produced public trust, and that economic strain and humanitarian need remain severe. Human Rights Watch, The Syria Report and Al-Jumhuriya converge in flagging gender-based exclusion in education and security institutions as a recurring and documented pattern rather than an isolated incident.

Where they differ

Sources diverge on how to structure political inclusion: the Atlantic Council warns against ethno-sectarian power-sharing as a recipe for deadlock, while ENKS and Kurdish outlets like Rudaw and Kurdpress press for guaranteed, formally recognised Kurdish representation, implying a more group-based model. JNS.org frames Syria's regional diplomacy, including with Yemen, as strategic security entrepreneurship, a more favourable reading than the Security Council's emphasis on fragility and unmet humanitarian needs.

Recommendations

  • The UN deputy envoy calls on the international community to turn goodwill into tangible benefits for Syria's transition rather than rhetorical support.
  • Plan International calls on the Security Council and donors to ensure urgent access to education for Syrian children.
  • Human Rights Watch calls on Syrian authorities to end the barring of women from defense and security colleges.
  • The Atlantic Council recommends Syrian political actors avoid ethno-sectarian power-sharing models to prevent the deadlock seen in Iraq and Lebanon.
  • ENKS urges organisers of meetings concerning Kurds to ensure inclusive and balanced representation.

Compared with what Syrians said

Syrian online discussion diverged sharply from the expert agenda in both focus and tone. The dominant story by far was the arrest of massacre suspect Mihrac Ural, generating celebration and calls for revenge, a justice theme absent from the UN and think-tank material reviewed here, which instead emphasised elections, institutions and humanitarian conditions. Property expropriation drew intense negative reaction (tone -0.62), a governance grievance tied to lived economic experience that none of the international items directly addressed, while a pro-government commentator drew a strongly positive wave of support (tone 0.6), reflecting polarised domestic framing of the authorities largely absent from the more technocratic international reporting.
